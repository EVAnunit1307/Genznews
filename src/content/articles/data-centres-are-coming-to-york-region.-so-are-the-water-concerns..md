Artificial intelligence is transforming the way we work, learn, and communicate. But behind every AI prompt, cloud storage request, and chatbot response lies a growing network of data centres—and in York Region, that growth is raising serious questions about the future.

A cluster of existing and proposed data centres across **Vaughan, Markham, and Richmond Hill** is forecasted to consume **up to 22 million litres of water every day**—enough to meet the daily needs of approximately **100,000 residents**.

The figures were brought forward by **Richmond Hill Councillor Carol Davidson**, whose motion calling on York Region to assess the long-term implications of data centre growth passed at Richmond Hill City Council and is set to be considered by York Regional Council in September.

> _"York Region is becoming one of Ontario's largest data centre hubs,"_ Davidson wrote on her website. _"Today, there are at least six operational centres, four under construction and another three proposed or planned across the region."_

According to Davidson, industry estimates suggest these facilities could require **more than 500 megawatts of electrical capacity over the next decade**—enough to fundamentally reshape the region's infrastructure.

## More Than Just Water

Data centres are often described as the backbone of the digital economy. They power artificial intelligence, cloud computing, online banking, streaming services, and nearly every digital platform we use every day.

But they also consume enormous amounts of resources.

To prevent thousands of high-performance servers from overheating, many facilities rely on water-intensive cooling systems. Others require vast amounts of electricity to operate around the clock. As AI models become larger and more complex, those demands are only expected to grow.

One proposed facility near **Leslie Street and Orlando Avenue** alone could consume **2.4 million litres of water every day**—roughly the same amount used daily by **10,000 residents**.

Construction company **Urbacon** already operates three data centres in York Region, has another under construction, and plans two more as part of its Barker Digital Campus.

The concern isn't a single facility.

It's the cumulative impact of all of them.

## The Question Isn't "Should We Have Data Centres?"

Councillor Davidson has been clear that her motion is **not** about opposing technology or AI investment.

Instead, she's asking whether York Region has fully considered what happens when every approved and proposed data centre is added together.

Can our water systems support this level of demand?

Can Ontario's electrical grid keep pace?

How will municipalities fund the infrastructure upgrades needed to support continued expansion?

These are questions that become increasingly important as York Region positions itself as one of Ontario's largest technology hubs.

## It's Not Just York Region

Communities across the Greater Toronto and Hamilton Area are beginning to ask similar questions.

In **Scarborough**, residents have expressed concerns over the expansion of Stack Infrastructure's data centre, citing noise, emissions, and environmental impacts.

In **Mississauga**, a proposed hyperscale data centre has sparked debate over land use, electricity demand, and whether municipalities are prepared for the rapid pace of AI-driven development.

As artificial intelligence accelerates, local governments are increasingly being asked to balance economic opportunity with environmental sustainability.

## Why This Matters for Gen Z

For many young people, artificial intelligence represents opportunity.

It promises new careers, faster innovation, medical breakthroughs, and economic growth.

But Gen Z will also inherit the long-term consequences of the infrastructure powering that future.

Today's decisions about water allocation, electricity generation, and industrial development will shape communities for decades.

If millions of litres of drinking water are committed to cooling servers every day, what happens during future droughts or periods of water scarcity?

If electricity demand from AI continues to surge, will Ontario need new transmission lines, additional generating stations, or increased reliance on fossil fuels during peak demand?

And who ultimately pays for those infrastructure investments?

Most likely, it's today's younger generation through future taxes, utility bills, and environmental trade-offs.

The challenge isn't choosing between AI and sustainability.

It's ensuring that technological progress doesn't come at the expense of the very communities expected to live with its consequences.

Artificial intelligence may define the future.

But how we choose to power it will define ours.
