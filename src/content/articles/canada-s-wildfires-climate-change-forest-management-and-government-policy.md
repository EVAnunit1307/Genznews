Canada has experienced several devastating wildfire seasons in recent years, with millions of hectares of forest burned and thousands of residents forced to evacuate. While climate change has become central to the conversation, wildfire experts stress that the issue is far more complex than simply blaming rising global temperatures.

Climate change is making many parts of Canada warmer and, in some regions, drier. These conditions can lengthen the wildfire season, reduce moisture in forests, and allow fires to spread more rapidly once they begin. However, climate change does **not** directly ignite wildfires. Most Canadian wildfires are started by **lightning** or **human activity**, including unattended campfires, sparks from equipment, power lines, and discarded cigarettes. Climate change influences the conditions that make fires more severe, rather than acting as the source of ignition.

Forest management also plays a significant role. For decades, fire suppression policies have prevented many naturally occurring low-intensity fires from clearing dead vegetation. As a result, forests in many regions have accumulated dense fuel loads that can contribute to larger, more intense wildfires when fires do occur. Fire scientists increasingly recommend prescribed burns, forest thinning, and other fuel-management practices alongside climate action to reduce wildfire risk.

Although many people assume Canada is experiencing far more wildfires than in previous decades, the data tells a more nuanced story. The **number of wildfires reported annually has remained relatively stable over the long term and has even declined in some years**. What has changed more dramatically is the **total area burned**. Recent wildfire seasons have seen fires grow much larger because of prolonged dry conditions, heavy fuel accumulation, and extreme weather events, making them harder to contain.

Government policy has also become part of the discussion. In Ontario, Premier Doug Ford's government has faced criticism from environmental organizations, former officials, and opposition parties over reductions in funding for conservation authorities and broader environmental programs, as well as changes to natural resource management priorities. Critics argue that these funding decisions reduced the province's capacity for long-term wildfire prevention, forest management, and ecosystem monitoring, placing greater emphasis on emergency response rather than prevention. While Ontario continues to fund wildfire suppression through Aviation, Forest Fire and Emergency Services, critics contend that responding to fires after they begin is not a substitute for sustained investment in prevention and forest resilience.

The Ford government rejects claims that its policies are responsible for Ontario's wildfire challenges, stating that it has continued to invest in firefighting personnel, aircraft, equipment, and emergency preparedness. However, many experts note that effective wildfire management requires consistent investment in both **prevention** and **suppression**, as well as coordinated planning between provincial governments, conservation authorities, Indigenous communities, and forestry experts. Canada's wildfire crisis cannot be attributed to a single cause. Climate change is creating conditions that increase the risk of large and destructive fires, but lightning, human activity, forest fuel accumulation, land management practices, and government policy all play important roles. Understanding how these factors interact is essential for developing effective wildfire strategies that protect both people and the environment.
