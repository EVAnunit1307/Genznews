It started as a way to prove a point to a friend who said cities "aren't doing anything." I wanted to show her they were. So I downloaded every climate commitment my city had published since 2019 — forty-one of them — and read all of them.

I was wrong, and so was she. The truth is more boring and more damning than either of us guessed.

## The targets are genuinely good

Let me start where the optimists are right. The pledges themselves are serious documents. Net-zero municipal operations by 2035. A tree canopy target with actual numbers. Bike infrastructure goals measured in kilometers, not vibes. Somebody smart wrote these, and they meant it.

If you only read the targets, you'd think we were saved.

> The gap between what a city promises and what a city funds is where the entire climate fight actually lives — and nobody reports on the funding.

## Then you get to the budgets

Here's the pattern that showed up in binder two. For nearly every ambitious target, the funding attached to it was a fraction of what the target required — sometimes a rounding error against the general budget. The goals were written in the language of emergency. The money was allocated in the language of "we'll see."

I made a list of the worst gaps:

1. A retrofit program funded for one year, with targets stretching to 2040.
2. A transit expansion "committed to" in a press release and absent from three consecutive budgets.
3. A resilience fund whose entire annual allocation was less than the city spent repainting road lines.

None of this is corruption. That's what makes it hard. It's just the ordinary physics of politics — targets are cheap and popular, budgets are expensive and boring, and the people who'd hold you to the difference aren't paying attention.

## What actually moves it

The two targets that were on track had one thing in common: a specific person whose job depended on hitting them, and a public dashboard that made falling behind visible. That's it. Not ideology. Not a bigger pledge. Accountability with a name attached and a number nobody could hide.

So when someone tells you your city is or isn't "doing something" about the climate, ask a different question. Don't ask what it promised. Ask what it funded, and who gets fired if it misses. The answer to that is the only forecast that matters.
