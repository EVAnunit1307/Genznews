Climate change is one of the most significant global challenges of the 21st century, and Canada plays a unique role in both contributing to the problem and responding to it. As a large, resource-rich country with a relatively small population, Canada produces a higher level of greenhouse gas emissions per person than many other countries while also experiencing many of the consequences of a warming climate.

Canada is responsible for roughly **1.5% of global greenhouse gas emissions**—a relatively small share compared to countries such as China, the United States, and India. However, on a **per-capita basis**, Canadians emit considerably more greenhouse gases than the global average. This is largely due to Canada's cold climate, vast geography, transportation needs, and energy-intensive industries, particularly oil and gas production.

The energy sector remains Canada's largest source of emissions. Provinces such as Alberta and Saskatchewan rely heavily on oil and natural gas for employment and economic growth, creating an ongoing debate between environmental protection and economic prosperity. Supporters argue that Canadian energy is produced under stricter environmental regulations than in many other countries and will remain essential during the global energy transition. Critics argue that continued investment in fossil fuels makes it more difficult for Canada to meet its climate commitments and slows the shift toward cleaner energy.

In response, Canada has implemented policies such as carbon pricing, methane reduction regulations, investments in renewable energy, and incentives for electric vehicles and clean technology. The federal government has also committed to achieving **net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050**, with many provinces expanding renewable energy, hydroelectricity, and nuclear power to reduce emissions.

Climate change is already affecting Canada. The country is warming at approximately **twice the global average**, with even faster warming in northern regions. These changes have contributed to longer wildfire seasons, more frequent heat waves, melting permafrost, changing precipitation patterns, and coastal erosion. However, it is important to recognize that climate change is only one part of the equation. Factors such as forest management, land-use planning, infrastructure, and natural climate variability also influence the severity of many environmental events. Addressing these challenges requires a combination of emissions reductions, adaptation strategies, and effective resource management.

## Why This Matters for Gen Z

For Generation Z, climate change is not simply an environmental issue—it is an economic, political, and social one. Young Canadians will inherit the long-term consequences of today's policy decisions while entering a workforce and economy that are rapidly changing.

The transition to cleaner energy is expected to create new opportunities in renewable energy, engineering, environmental science, sustainable finance, and green technology. At the same time, industries that rely heavily on fossil fuels may experience significant changes, requiring workers to adapt to new skills and careers. As governments invest in climate initiatives, Canadians may also see higher costs associated with carbon pricing, infrastructure projects, and the transition to cleaner technologies, while benefiting from new investments in public transportation, energy efficiency, and innovation.

Climate change will also influence where people live, how cities are built, insurance costs, food prices, and public health. More frequent extreme weather events can disrupt communities and increase the cost of maintaining homes, infrastructure, and emergency services. These economic pressures will shape the financial future of many young Canadians, particularly as they navigate challenges such as housing affordability and the rising cost of living.

For Gen Z, the climate conversation is ultimately about balance. Reducing emissions, protecting the environment, maintaining economic competitiveness, and ensuring affordable energy are all interconnected goals. The challenge for Canada's next generation of leaders will be finding practical solutions that protect both the environment and the opportunities available to future Canadians.

Canada's role in climate change is therefore both a responsibility and an opportunity. The decisions made today will shape not only the country's environmental future but also the careers, communities, and quality of life of Generation Z for decades to come.

## What Canada Needs to Do Moving Forward

Addressing climate change will require Canada to move beyond debate and focus on practical solutions that balance environmental responsibility with economic stability. Because Canada’s emissions come from multiple sectors, there is no single policy that can solve the issue. Instead, the country will need a combination of strategies involving government, industry, and communities.

First, Canada must continue reducing emissions from the energy sector while recognizing the economic importance of natural resources. This includes investing in cleaner technologies, reducing methane emissions, improving carbon capture and storage where appropriate, and supporting the development of renewable energy sources. Canada’s ability to produce energy with lower emissions could help maintain economic competitiveness while contributing to global climate goals.

Second, Canada needs stronger investments in adaptation and resilience. Climate change impacts such as wildfires, floods, and extreme heat are already affecting communities, meaning governments must improve emergency preparedness, infrastructure, forest management, and disaster planning. Preventing damage before it occurs can reduce future costs and protect Canadians.

Third, Canada must focus on innovation and creating opportunities for younger generations. Investments in clean technology, research, skilled trades, and sustainable industries can help create new careers for Gen Z while ensuring Canada remains competitive in a changing global economy. Climate policy should not only focus on reducing emissions but also on preparing Canadians for the jobs and industries of the future.

Finally, Canada must approach climate change with a balanced and evidence-based perspective. Effective solutions require cooperation between federal and provincial governments, Indigenous communities, businesses, scientists, and citizens. Climate action will be most successful when it considers both environmental goals and the economic realities faced by Canadians.

For Generation Z, the question is not whether climate change matters—it is how Canada can respond in a way that protects the environment while creating a stable, affordable, and prosperous future. The decisions made today will shape the opportunities available to young Canadians for decades to come.
